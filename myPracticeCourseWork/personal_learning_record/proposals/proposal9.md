# Council Proposal 9

## Explanation of this proposal
*What do you think this proposal might mean? How might it be implemented?*

The proposal is about strengthening the council’s cybersecurity posture to defend against cyber crime. This includes protecting sensitive citizen data, safeguarding IT infrastructure, and ensuring continuity of services in the face of growing digital threats. It complements earlier proposals like Proposal 6 (digital‑first services) and Proposal 8 (AI‑driven operations). As the council moves more of its services online and relies on advanced technologies, the risk of cyber attacks increases. Hardening security is therefore a necessary foundation for modernization. Implementing stronger authentication and access controls.

Regular patching and vulnerability management.

Security awareness training for staff.

Incident response planning and disaster recovery strategies.

Monitoring for threats such as phishing, ransomware, and denial‑of‑service attacks. Protects citizen trust, ensures compliance with data protection laws, reduces risk of service disruption. Position the council as a secure, reliable digital service provider; build resilience against future threats. Proposal 9 is about making cybersecurity a core priority so that all the other modernization initiatives (cloud migration, digital services, AI, low‑code platforms) can operate safely and sustainably. The proposal likely means that the council wants to make its website and mobile app the main way the public interacts with council services. In practice, this means:

Most service requests, payments, reports, applications, and information searches would be done online first.

The website and app would be designed as the primary “front door” for residents. Other channels such as phone lines, email, and in-person visits would still exist but would be secondary options, mainly for people who cannot use digital services.

The council aims to improve efficiency, reduce costs, speed up responses, and provide a more consistent and convenient experience.

Services would be redesigned around digital processes rather than traditional paper-based or manual ones. Reviewing all existing services and converting them into clear online processes.

Replacing paper forms with digital forms and self-service tools.

Standardising processes across departments so residents have a consistent experience. Improving the council’s website to make it easy to navigate and fully accessible.

Developing a mobile app that allows residents to report issues, make payments, receive updates, and track requests.

Ensuring both platforms meet accessibility standards (e.g., WCAG compliance). Connecting the website/app with internal council systems so requests flow directly to the correct team.

Introducing automated workflows to reduce manual handling.

Setting up dashboards so residents can track progress (e.g., bin request status, report-a-problem updates). Promoting the website and app through communications, signage, emails, and social media.

Highlighting the benefits of using digital channels (speed, convenience, 24/7 access).

Guiding residents toward self-service before offering alternative channels. Keeping phone and in-person options available for those who need them.

Offering training or help sessions for residents who lack digital skills.

Installing public access kiosks in libraries or council buildings. Using data from the digital platforms to identify what works well and where improvements are needed.

Updating the website and app regularly.

Adjusting services based on public feedback and usage patterns.

The cybercrime landscape has changed considerably since the first edition of Cybercrime: The 
Transformation of Crime in the Information Age was published in 2007 and yet the field is still as 
contradictory as it was back then. On the one hand, new technologies have created entirely new or 
extended criminal opportunities for cybercrime, yet on the other hand, there have also been some 
major advances in policing and cybersecurity. But old issues relating to the initial problems that 
cybercrime created for regulating and policing it keep raising their ugly head in addition to entirely 
new challenges. Below, is an overview of the changes and issues which frame this second edition and 
also a chapter outline.  The cybercrime field has changed technologically since the early 2000s following the advent of social 
media, cloud technologies, the ‘Internet of Things’, botnets and cryptocurrencies. These developments 
have led to a marked step-change in the cyber-threat landscape, not least, the way that social 
engineering and malware (malicious software) are now combined, in addition ‘information-guzzling 
malware’ (to quote The Register) such as Zeus, Emotet and Trickbot are deployed via botnets and,

ransomware, data extortion and ransomhacks have become dominant threats in addition to frauds. 
Moreover, as the importance and value of data has increased for its owners, then cybercrimes 
involving data breaches are now commonplace and should be better thought of as informational 
crimes. While many of the previously unfounded claims about the impact of cybercrime, about which I was 
once sceptical, are now starting to come true, it is nevertheless the case that governments, policing 
agencies and cybersecurity have since become more adept at responding to cybercrimes in terms of 
their prevention, mitigation and investigation. They have increased their individual cyber-security 
capacity and developed national and international cybersecurity policies and response arrangements. 
But these measures have clearly not been enough because police still seem to find themselves one 
step behind the cybercriminals. Police organisations still seem to lack responsive capacity and the 
world’s governments still seem to be slow in providing a major and collective response. Part of the 
problem is that Cybercrime, like fraud, does not ‘bang, bleed or shout’ (to quote a senior UK police officer) so fails to receive the political priority given to more visceral or violent forms of offending. All 
the while, misunderstandings still persist about what cybercrimes are and how they impact upon 
society. For various practical reasons, Cybercrimes are still largely underreported by victims and are

taking over the internet or that that hackers have becomes super powerful and that powerful nation
